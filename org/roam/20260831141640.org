* Introduction

Graphs capture pairwise relationships between objects. A graph is a pair $\left(V , E\right)$, where
+ $V$ is a vertex set
+ $E$ is the edge set: a set of pairs of vertices, $E \subseteq \binom{V}{2}$
  - $u , v \in V : \left\{u , v\right\} \in E$
  - Edges may also be denoted as simply $u v \in E$

  We often denote $n = | V |$, $m = | E |$

* Representation

** Edge list

Keep $E$ as a list of pairs.
+ Space $O\left(m\right)$
+ Iterate over edges: $O\left(m\right)$ time, $O\left(1\right)$ per edge
+ Check if $u , v \in V$ are adjacent: $O\left(m\right)$ time
+ Iterate over neigjbors of $u \in V$: $O\left(m\right)$ time

** Adjacency matrix

Store graph as an $n \times n$ matrix $A$, $A_{u v} = 1 \Leftrightarrow u v \in E$.
+ Space: $O\left(n^2\right)$
+ Iterate over edges: $O\left(n^2\right)$ time
+ Check if $u , v \in V$ are adjacent: $O\left(1\right)$ time
+ Iterate over neighbors of $u \in V$: $O\left(n\right)$ time

- Works well on dense graphs $m = \Theta \left(n^2\right)$
- Not so good on sparse graphs $m = \Theta(n)$

** Adjacency lists

For each vertex, store the list of the neighbors.
+ Space: $O\left(m + n\right)$
+ Iterate over all edges: $O\left(m + n\right)$ time
+ Check if $u , v \in V$ are adjacent: $O\left(\deg u\right)$ time (or log / constant time depending on implementation, such as an ordered set or a hash set)
+ Iterate over neighbors of $u \in V$: $O\left(\deg u\right)$ time, $O\left(1\right)$ per neighbors.

** Paths and connectivity

Definition: A *path* in $G$ is a sequence of vertices $v_1 , v_2 , \ldots , v_k$ where each consecutive pair $v_{i - 1} v_i$ is jouned by a different edge $E$. A path is *simple* if all its verticaes are distinct. A graph is *connected* if for every pair of vertices $u , v \in V$ there is a path between $u$ and $v$.

** Cycles

Definition: A *cycle* in $G$ is a path $v_1 , v_2 , \ldots , v_k$ where $v_1 = v_k$ and $k \geq 2$. A cycle is *simple* if all of its nodes are distinct (except $v_1$ and $v_k$). A graph is *acyclic* if it has no cycles.

** Trees

Definition: A graph $G$ is a *tree* if it is connected and acyclic.

Lemma: Let $G$ be a graph on $n$ vertices. Any two of the following statements imply the third:
+ $G$ is connected
+ $G$ does not contain a cycle
+ $G$ has $n - 1$ edges

*** Proof

Given:
+ $G$ is connected
+ $G$ has no cycles
    $| E | = n - 1$

Base: For a graph with $n = 1$, there will only be one node, so there are no edges.
$n \rightarrow n + 1$: we add $1$ edge to connect the new vertex
Given a forest of multiple trees: $1 + n_1 + \ldots + n_k = 1 + n$, by induction hypothesis: $k + \left(n - 1\right) + \left(n_2 - 1\right) + \ldots + \left(n_k - 1\right) = n$

** Common problems

+ $s$-$t$ *connectivity problem*: Given two vertices $s$ and $t$ in $G$, is there a path between $s$ and $t$?
  Can be precomputed in $O\left(m \alpha(n)\right)$ and queried in $O\left(q \cdot \alpha(n)\right)$ where $q$ is the number of queries and $n$ is the number of nodes using DSU.
+ $s$-$t$ *shortest path problem*: Given two vertices $s$ and $t$ in $G$, what is the length of a shortest path between $s$ and $t$?
  Can be precomputed in $O\left(m + n\right)$ (DFS from root) and queried in $O\left(\log n\right)$ (LCM).

Applications:
+ Physically reach $A$ from $B$
+ Compute Erdös number
+ Send a packet over network
+ Fill area with the same color

* Algorithms

** Breadth-first search

Intuition: explore outwards from $s$ in all directions simultaneously. The algorithm computes a sequence of layers:

\begin{aligned}
L_0 & = \left\{s\right\} \\
 L_1 & = \text{all neighbors of} \; s \\
 L_2 & = \text{all neighbors of} \; L_1 \; \text{that are nto already in} \; L_0 \; \text{or} \; L_1 \\
 L_{i + 1} & = \text{all vertices having a neighbor in} \; L_i \; \text{that are not already in} \; L_0 , \ldots , L_i
\end{aligned}

#+DOWNLOADED: screenshot @ 2026-08-31 15:50:23
#+attr_org: :width 400px
[[file:./assets/Algorithms/20260831155023_screenshot.png]]

Observation: $L_i$ consists of vertices exactly at distance $i$ from $s$. There is a path from $s$ to $t$ if $t$ appears in some layer.

#+begin_quote
Claim: Let $uv$ be an edge of $G$. The layers of $u$ anv $v$ differ by at most one.
+ Assume $u \in L_i$ and $v \in L_j$, where $j \geq i + 2$
+ By definition of $L_j$, $v$ is not in $L_i$ or lower
+ Hence, $v$ must be in $L_i + 1$ - contradiction!
#+end_quote

If we want to keep track of the layers, we may also increment a variable $i$ and keep track of layers as individual lists. Equivalently, we can maintain a =vector<int> dist= instead of =vector<bool> visited=. The BFS algorithm runs in time $O\left(m + n\right)$.
