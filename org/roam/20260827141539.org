* Supervised learning

Given collections of experience / data in the form $\left(\text{input} , \text{output}\right)$, we want to learn some $\text{output} = \text{model}\left(\text{input}\right)$, which mathematically is $Y = f\left(X\right)$ or $f : X \rightarrow Y$.


We distinguish SL by the type of inputs-outputs:

+ *Univariate* (single-input) *single-output*
+ *Multivariate* (many-input) *single-output*
+ *Univariate* (single-input) *multiple-output*
+ *Multivariate* (many-input) *single-output*

** Univariate Linear Regression

This uses simple and widely studied models, and is often computationally convenient.

Univariate linear regression maps one feature to one outcome ($y = f\left(x\right)$). We often express this as a map: $f : \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$. It imposes a specific form onto this generic definition. The function $f\left(x\right)$ will be described by two parameters:

+ *Slope* $w$ specifying the angle of the line
+ *Intercept* $b$ specifying the displacement of the line

In addition, we have a term $\epsilon$ accounting for the *error* (or *residual*) of the observation

\[
y = w \cdot x + b + \epsilon
\]

*** Risk minimization

Choosing $w$ and $b$. *Empirical risk minimization* (ERM) minimizes a /loss/ on training data. A loss function $L\left(y , f\left(x\right)\right)$ is a measure of the "cost" of predicting $f\left(x\right)$ when the true label is $y$.

+ $L\left(y , f\left(x\right)\right) = 0$ only if $y = f\left(x\right)$
+ The loss increases when $y$ and $f\left(x\right)$ are far apart

In regression, a common loss function is the minimization of the sum of squared errors on the training set:

\begin{aligned}
L\left(x , f\left(y\right)\right) & = \sum_{i = 1}^n \left(y_i - f\left(x_i\right)\right)^2 \\
 & = \sum_{i = 0}^n \left(y_i - \left(w x_i + b\right)\right)^2 \\
 & = \sum_{i = 0}^n \epsilon_i^2 \\
 & = E\left(w , b\right)
\end{aligned}

Estimates for the parameters can be found by solving

\[
\left(\widehat{w} , \widehat{b}\right) = \arg \min_{w , b} E\left(w , b\right)
\]

Minimizing the sum of squared errors is now equivalent to minimizing the mean squared error:

\[
L\left(y , f\left(x\right)\right)) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i = 1}^n \left(y_i - \left(w x_i + b\right)\right)^2
\]

*** Solving the optimization problem

We want to minimize $E\left(w , b\right)$ by choosing values for parameters $w$ and $b$. In general, there are two ways to approach this problem:

+ *Analytical approach*: find mathematically an exact close solution
+ *Numericall / Approximate approach*: rely on algorithms / heuristics leading to a good solution

Linear regression is solvable analytically:

1. Compute partial derivatives (gradient)

\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial E\left(w , b\right)}{\partial b} & = \frac{\partial \sum_{i = 1}^n \left(y_i - \left(w x_i + b\right)\right)^2}{\partial b} \\
 & = \sum_{i = 1}^n 2\left(y_i - w x_i - b\right) \cdot \left(- 1\right) \\
 & = - 2 \sum_{i = 1}^n 2\left(y_i - w x_i - b\right) \\
 \frac{\partial E\left(w , b\right)}{\partial w} & = \frac{\partial \sum_{i = 1}^n \left(y_i - \left(w x_i + b\right)\right)^2}{\partial w} \\
 & = \sum_{i = 1}^n 2\left(y_i - w x_i - b\right) \cdot \left(- x_i\right) \\
 & = - 2 \sum_{i = 1}^n x_{i\left(y_i - w x_i - b\right)}
\end{aligned}

2. Set partial derivatives to zero

\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial E\left(w , b\right)}{\partial b} & = - 2 \sum_{i = 1}^n \left(y_i - w x_i - b\right) = 0 \\
 \frac{\partial E\left(w , b\right)}{\partial w} & = - 2 \sum_{i = 1}^n x_i \left(y_i - w x_i - b\right) = 0
\end{aligned}

3. Solve for $\widehat{b}$:

\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial E\left(w , b\right)}{\partial b} & = - 2 \sum_{i = 1}^n \left(y_i - w x_i - b\right) = 0 \\
 \widehat{b} & = \overline{y} - w \overline{x}
\end{aligned}

4. Solve for $\widehat{w}$:

\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial E\left(w , b\right)}{\partial w} = - 2 \sum_{i = 1}^n x_i \left(y_i - w x_i - b\right) & = 0 \\
 \sum_{i = 1}^n x_i \left(y_i - w x_i - b\right) & = 0 \\
 \sum_{i = 1}^n x_i \left(y_i - w x_i - \overline{y} + w \overline{x}\right) & = 0 \\
 \sum_{i = 1}^n x_i \left(y_i - \overline{y}\right) - w \sum_{i = 1}^n x_i \left(x_i - \overline{x}\right) & = 0 \\
 \sum_{i = 1}^n x_i \left(y_i - \overline{y}\right) & = w \sum_{i = 1}^n x_i \left(x_i - \overline{x}\right) \\
 \widehat{w} & = \frac{\sum_{i = 1}^n x_i \left(y_i - \overline{y}\right)}{(\sum_{i = 1}^n x_i \left(x_i - \overline{x}\right)}
\end{aligned}

Now, we can use an algebraic identity which says that the sum of deviations from the mean $\sum \left(x_i - \overline{x}\right)) = 0$. Due to this, we can subtract it from the first term without changing the value, transforming $x_i$ info $\left(x_i - \overline{x}\right)$.

\begin{aligned}
\widehat{w} & = \frac{\sum_{i = 1}^n \left(x_i - \overline{x}\right)\left(y_i - \overline{y}\right)}{\sum_{i = 1}^n \left(x_i - \overline{x}\right)^2}
\end{aligned}

Now, notice that the numerator is the sample covariance of $x$ and $y$, while the denominator is the sample variance of $x$:

\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{p q} & = \frac{1}{n - 1} \sum_{i = 1}^n \left(p_i - \overline{p}\right) \left(q_i - \overline{q}\right)
\end{aligned}

Thus, $\widehat{w} = \frac{\sigma_{x y}}{\sigma_{x x}}$

** Logistic regression

Using linear regression can be problematic when predicting categorical responses, so we can use a variant known as /logistic regression/. This provides a way to use linear models in classification tasks.

#+attr_org: :width 400px
[[file:./assets/Supervised_learning/20260908203053_screenshot.png]]


Consider a binary classification problem $y_i \in \left\{0 , 1\right\}$. The /prediction probabillity/ is defined as $p = P\left(y = 1 | x\right)$. Logistic linear regression interprets linear regression as the computation of a /log-odds ratio/ $\log \frac{p}{1 - p} \mathrm{\mathbf{w}}^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}}$. This is equivalent to $P\left(y = 1 |\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}\right) = \sigma \left(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}}\right)$, where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the /logistic sigmoid/ $\sigma(z) = \frac{e^z}{1 + e^z} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{- z}}$. A sigmoid function looks like this:

#+DOWNLOADED: screenshot @ 2026-09-08 20:42:43
#+attr_org: :width 400px
[[file:./assets/Supervised_learning/20260908204243_screenshot.png]]

When used in classification, the decision boundary is defined by $P\left(y = 1 |\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}\right) = P\left(y = 0 |\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}\right) = 0.5$. This corresponds to a hyperplane $\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}} = 0$. Hence, the classification rule is

+ $\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}} > 0 \rightarrow y = 1$
+ $\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}} < 0 \rightarrow y = 0$

We want to maximize the probability of the true class label $y$ given the features $\mathbf{X}$.

*** Solving logistic regression

The conditional likelihood of labels $\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}$ given $\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}$ can be expressed as:

\begin{aligned}
P\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{y}} |\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}\right) & = \prod_{i = 1}^n P\left(y_i | x_i\right) \\
 & = \prod_{i = 1}^n P\left(y_i = 1 | x_i\right)^{y_i} P\left(y_i = 0 | x_i\right)^{1 - y_i} \\
 & = \prod_{i = 1}^n P\left(y_i = 1 | x_i\right)^{y_i} \left(1 - P\left(y_i = 1 | x_i\right)\right)^{1 - y_i} \\
 & = \prod_{i = 1}^n \sigma(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_i)^{y_i} \left(1 - \sigma(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_i)\right)^{1 - y_i}
\end{aligned}

Then maximizing the likelihood $\displaystyle \prod_{i = 1}^n \sigma(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_i)^{y_i} \left(1 - \sigma(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_i)\right)^{1 - y_i}$ is equivalent to maximizing the /log-likelihood/:

\[
\operatorname{scr}(l l) \left(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}\right) = \sum_{i = 1}^n \left(y_i \log \sigma(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_i) + \left(1 - y_i\right) \log \left(1 - \sigma(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_i)\right)\right)
\]

This again, is equivalent to minimizing the /negative log-likelihood/

\[
L \left(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}\right) = -\sum_{i = 1}^n \left(y_i \log \sigma(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_i) + \left(1 - y_i\right) \log \left(1 - \sigma(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_i)\right)\right)
\]

The log-loss function does not have an /analytic close-form solution/. However, it is convex and it is possible to use an iterative /gradient-based/ method.

*** Gradient descent

*Gradient descent* is an approximate method for finding a local minimum / maximum of a multivariate function.

#+begin_quote
Let $f\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}\right)$ be a differentiable function. Then, in the neighborhood of point $\mathrm{\mathbf{a}}$, $f\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}\right)$ decreases fastest if one goes to the direction of the negative [[id:933639AF-2E30-480F-8518-EA01ED4330C3][gradient]] of $f\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}\right)$, that is, $- \nabla f\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{a}}\right)$
#+end_quote

Iteratively, we have $\mathrm{\mathbf{a}}_{t + 1} = \mathrm{\mathbf{a}}_t - \gamma_t \nabla f\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{a}}_t\right)$. Here, $\gamma$ is a hyperparameter for the /learning rate/ (step size). If the value of $\gamma$ is too small, convergence takes too long. *** Using gradient descent with logistic regression

We need to find the parameters $\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}$ that minimize the loss function.

The derivative of the sigmoid function is $\sigma'\left(z\right) = \sigma(z) \left(1 - \sigma(z)\right)$, and the gradient of the log-loss is $\displaystyle \nabla L\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}\right) = \sum_{i = 1}^n \mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_i \left(\sigma(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_i) - y_i\right)$. Iteratively, $\displaystyle \mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{t + 1} = \mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_t - \gamma_t \sum_{i = 1}^n \mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_i \left(\sigma(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_t^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_i) - y_I\right)$.

*** Multiclass logistic regression

Instead of using a /sigmoid/ $P\left(y_i = 1 |\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_i\right) = \sigma(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_i)$, we rely on a /softmax/ $P\left(y_I = k |\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_i\right) = \frac{\exp^{\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_i}}{\sum_j \exp^{\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}^T \mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_j}}$.
